How to study this guide

Allow 45–50 minutes for reading and study. Use about 12 minutes for chapters 1–3 (pipeline, evaluation, team and value), 10 minutes for IoT and edge computing, 10 minutes for text and sentiment, 8 minutes for automated processes and 8 minutes for the worked answer and review. Pause after each chapter and say which decision it supports. The quiz and flashcards come after the reading.

The real-world applications below extend the course concepts.

Every block asks one question: which decision changes, and how will we know it improved? Data becomes a model, a model gives a score, a score triggers an action, and the action needs a measure. A model is a rule learned from data that produces a prediction. A baseline is a trivial rule the model must beat to be worth anything.

1. A model is a chain: pipeline, features and honest splits

A pipeline is a chain of steps that carries data from collection to a model that works every day: data collection, preprocessing (cleaning and reshaping), model training, evaluation and deployment with monitoring. It is a loop: a weak evaluation sends you back to the data, and a drop in production sends you back to training. CRISP-DM, built by an industry consortium in the late 1990s, describes it in six phases from business understanding to deployment. CRISP-DM history and phases

Each row of historical data has features, the clues, and a label, the correct answer already known, such as "this booking was cancelled: yes or no". The model learns the link between them. In real use it sees only the clues. Feature engineering turns raw data into usable clues: from a booking date and a dinner date you can build "days in advance". Data leakage means the model uses information it would not have at prediction time. The score looks excellent and is false. Ask of every column: would I know this when I predict? A "refund issued" column in a model that predicts returns fails, because the refund comes after the return.

Labelled data is split into three piles, often about 70%, 15% and 15%. The training set is for learning. The validation set is for choosing between models and hyperparameters, settings fixed before training, such as the maximum depth of a tree. The test set is opened once, at the end. If you peek at it while you keep adjusting, it becomes a second training set and the final score turns optimistic. The split must copy reality. To forecast the future, train on the past and test on the latest period. Keep all rows of one customer on the same side.

Real case: a test fixed in advance. In the 2018 RecSys Challenge, models received incomplete Spotify playlists and had to suggest more tracks. On a separate evaluation set some tracks were hidden, and the score showed how many appeared among the recommendations and how high. The organisers decided in advance what the model must not see. The contest measured recovery of original tracks, not whether a new listener would enjoy them. Spotify Research, RecSys Challenge 2018

Apply it. A streetwear brand that predicts who will buy the next drop should train on earlier drops, test on the latest one and drop any column recorded after the drop, such as returns or reward points. It should also beat a baseline like "whoever bought the last drop buys again". Review: The variables matter more than the algorithm.

2. Accuracy can lie: precision, recall and F1

A confusion matrix counts the four outcomes of a classifier: TP (flagged and really positive), FP (a false alarm), FN (a real case missed) and TN (a correct "no"). Hypothetical restaurant predicting cancellations; "positive" means the guest cancels. Out of 100 bookings:

Really cancels Really comes
Model says: cancels TP = 15 FP = 10
Model says: comes FN = 5 TN = 70
  • Accuracy = (TP + TN) / total = 85 / 100 = 85%.
  • Precision = TP / (TP + FP) = 15 / 25 = 60%. Of the bookings flagged, how many really cancel.
  • Recall = TP / (TP + FN) = 15 / 20 = 75%. Of all real cancellations, how many are found.
  • F1 = 2 × P × R / (P + R) = 2 × 0.60 × 0.75 / 1.35 ≈ 66.7%. A harmonic mean: high only when both are high.

The accuracy trap. Here 20 of 100 bookings cancel. A lazy model that always says "comes" is wrong only on those 20. Its accuracy is 80% and its recall is 0%. When the cases you care about are rare (fraud, churn, faults), accuracy is almost useless. Compare with the baseline first.

Choose by cost of error. If a false alarm is cheap and a miss is expensive, push recall. If each alarm triggers a costly action, push precision. Hypothetical telecom: 50 of 1,000 customers will leave, each worth €500 of margin. An offer costs €20 and keeps half of those who were leaving. Model A flags 200 and finds 40 leavers (precision 20%, recall 80%). Model B flags 30 and finds 20 (precision 67%, recall 40%). B looks better on accuracy (96% against 83%) and F1 (50% against 32%). Now count money. A: cost 200 × €20 = €4,000, keeps 20 customers worth €10,000, net +€6,000. B: cost €600, keeps 10 worth €5,000, net +€4,400. A wins because a miss costs far more than a false alarm. Write the cost of each error before you pick the metric.

Overfitting means the model memorised the training data, noise included: low error on training data and high error on new data, for example 2% against 25%. Underfitting means it is too simple and fails on both. Cures for overfitting are a simpler model, regularisation (a penalty on complexity) or more representative data.

Real case: model drift at Zillow. Model drift is a fall in performance because the world changes after training. Zillow used a pricing algorithm to buy, renovate and resell homes (Zillow Offers). In 2021 the housing market became unstable and many homes were overvalued. On 2 November 2021 the board decided to wind the business down, citing the unpredictability of home prices. It recorded a $304 million inventory write-down in the third quarter and cut about 25% of staff. Zillow Group, Form 8-K, 2021 · Zillow Group, Form 10-K, FY2021

Quick review from 20 October. A decision tree asks a chain of questions on features. A node holds a question, a branch is an answer, a leaf holds the prediction. Trees are readable but overfit when deep, and small data changes can reshape them. A Chicago hospital's tree-like chest-pain rule raised the share of uncomplicated patients correctly sent to lighter wards from 21% to 36%. Reilly et al., JAMA, 2002 K-means groups cases without labels. You choose K. It places K centroids, assigns each point to the nearest, moves each centroid to the mean of its group and repeats until nothing changes. Scale the variables, try several K values and starts, and check that groups make sense.

Apply it. A bank deciding whether to freeze suspicious card payments should compare the cost of a missed fraud with the cost of a wrongly blocked card, then choose recall or precision. Review: Accuracy can lie and Memorising is not learning.

3. From a model to a team: owners, decisions and value

In the lesson's fictional company the CEO says: "We have dashboards everywhere and still do not understand why profit is falling." The first question is not "which tool do we buy?" but which business decision must change? A good analytical question names an action, an owner and a time horizon. "Profit is falling" becomes "which products, customers or channels explain the fall in margin?", owned by the CFO or sales, who then review prices or costs.

Every request enters through the same intake brief: decision and owner, expected result, users, data and constraints, acceptance criteria.

Choose use cases with a score. Rate each project from 1 to 5: value 40%, feasibility 25%, data readiness 20%, adoption 15%. Blocking constraints, such as mandatory controls, come first. They are gates, not points. Dashboard (scores 4, 5, 4, 5): 1.6 + 1.25 + 0.8 + 0.75 = 4.40. Churn model (5, 2, 2, 3): 2.0 + 0.5 + 0.4 + 0.45 = 3.35. Data platform (4, 2, 3, 2): 3.00. Churn has the highest value but weak data and feasibility, so it moves up only if new evidence shows the data is ready. The score is a tool, not a verdict, and changing the weights to favour a pet project defeats it.

Organise the team. A centralised team gives standards but becomes a queue. A decentralised one is close to local decisions but duplicates work and uses different definitions. Hub-and-spoke keeps a centre for platform, shared datasets, metric definitions and quality, with analysts placed in the business areas. A RACI matrix says, for each decision, who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted and Informed.

Deliver small and measure value. Work in short CRISP-DM cycles. The first release is small but complete: one decision, a few users, a narrow but reliable slice of data, a result inside an existing workflow. Plan 90 days: align, pilot, then decide to scale, revise or stop. Measure delivery (time to a usable result), trust (reconciliation errors), adoption (repeated use) and business result. A pilot with €80,000 of attributable benefit and €30,000 of extra cost nets €50,000. The critical question is what evidence makes the benefit "attributable". The best answer is a control group.

Resist surprises. If the CEO finds the dashboard unambitious and wants "AI", keep the dashboard people use and add a linked predictive pilot. If a quarter of customer records has no usable ID, repair the data, narrow the scope or change project.

Apply it. A fashion group with ten divisions and ten definitions of "active customer" needs hub-and-spoke: the centre fixes the definition, the spokes stay near each market. Review: The CEO has dashboards everywhere.

4. Where the machine thinks: edge, fog and cloud

The Internet of Things (IoT) is a network of physical objects with sensors, software and connectivity. It works in five steps: sense, connect, process, decide, act. A sensor measures the physical world; an actuator acts on it, for example by opening a valve. For a business the chain is sense → interpret → act → measure. Data creates value only when it supports a useful action. If no decision changes, sensors are just a cost.

Where is the data processed? Edge computing runs on the device or very near it and answers in milliseconds. Fog computing is a site-level layer, such as a factory gateway, that aggregates, coordinates, buffers and translates protocols. Cloud computing is central and holds history and the view of the whole fleet. Latency is the delay between event and response. The rule: the place of the computation follows the decision deadline, then connectivity, data sensitivity, power and lifecycle cost.

Worked latency budget. A decision must come within 50 ms. At the edge: 20 ms to capture, 15 ms to prepare and run the model, 15 ms to communicate and act. Total 50 ms, so it just fits. Add a cloud round trip of 120 ms and the total is 170 ms, 120 ms late. A stronger cloud model shortens the computing, not the journey.

Train in the cloud, run at the edge. Edge AI (inference) runs an already trained model. The cloud trains and improves models on the whole fleet. Every update must be tested, versioned, signed and reversible, and drift watched machine by machine. Store-and-forward buffering keeps priority events when the network drops and sends them on return. Test it: a buffer that fills in 20 minutes cannot survive a three-hour outage. Telemetry is the stream of measurements from a remote machine, and SCADA the system operators use to monitor and command plants. Analytics does not replace a certified safety controller.

Real case: Mirai, 2016. Mirai searched the internet for cameras and video recorders that still used factory-default usernames and passwords and turned them into a botnet. On 21 October 2016 it flooded Dyn, a DNS provider, and many well-known sites became unreachable for hours. A cheap device without a secure identity and updates is a risk for everyone. The defence: unique device identity, protected access, signed updates with rollback. KrebsOnSecurity, 2016 · US Department of Homeland Security, 2016

Real case: Siemens Amberg. A model trained in the cloud and run at the edge predicts which circuit boards risk defects, so only those are X-rayed. Siemens reported up to 30% fewer X-ray tests in 2019. This is a company claim for one application, not independently verified. Siemens and ZVEI, ETSI AI Summit, 2019

Payback period = investment / net annual benefit. Hypothetical wind pilot: investment €300k, avoided losses €240k a year, running costs €60k a year. Net benefit 240 − 60 = €180k. Payback 300 / 180 ≈ 1.7 years. Stress test: avoided losses halve to €120k, net benefit becomes €60k and payback 5 years. Halving the avoided loss triples the payback because running costs stay fixed. Also: power (MW) is now, energy (MWh) is over a stated period. 3 MW for 2 hours is 6 MWh.

A pilot deserves money when it has a baseline, a decision owner, a timely local action and a critical hypothesis. In the group task, the Dubai Challenge, a proposal states two business measures, one technical measure and one critical hypothesis, tests on representative assets (including network outages) and counts full lifecycle cost.

Apply it. A festival that monitors crowd density at its gates needs an alert in seconds, so the rule runs locally and the cloud only compares patterns across events. Review: Where the machine should think.

5. Text as data: the NLP pipeline

Unstructured data has no fixed rows and columns: reviews, emails, chats, transcripts. It often says why behind the numbers. Natural Language Processing (NLP) is the branch of AI that lets computers read and produce human language. Language is hard because it is ambiguous, redundant and full of metaphor, while formal languages mean exactly one thing.

A sentence passes five levels. Lexical: split into tokens ("He goes to college." gives five). Syntactic: is it well formed? Semantic: does the literal meaning make sense? Discourse: what do neighbouring sentences add (what does "that" refer to)? Pragmatic: what is the intention? "Can you close the window?" is a request, not a question about your abilities. If one level fails, the sentence is dropped or misread. Noam Chomsky's 1957 "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" is grammatical and meaningless: it passes syntax and fails semantics. Chomsky's example

Text is prepared in seven steps. Each is a choice, not a neutral rule.

Step Question Example
Segmentation Where does a sentence end? Two sentences in one comment
Tokenization What are the words? The · stars · are · twinkling
Stop-word removal Which words carry little meaning? Drop "the", "is", "at"
Stemming What is the root? skipping, skipped → skip
Lemmatization What is the dictionary form? am, are, is → be
POS tagging What role does each word play? "book a ticket" (verb), "a good book" (noun)
NER Which names, places, companies, sums? Ticketmaster → organisation

Stemming cuts endings with mechanical rules. It is fast but can produce non-words such as "studi". Lemmatization uses grammar and a dictionary: more precise, slower, almost essential in Italian. Stop words are frequent words with little meaning. Dropping them is fine for counting topics and dangerous for sentiment: many standard lists contain "not", so "The show was not good" becomes "show good" and the signal is gone.

Where NLP trips. NLP works well for chatbots, translation and search. Sarcasm is a pragmatic problem: "Great idea to put the button under the banner. Well done." is literally positive and really a complaint, and the first three levels let it through. Domain shift is another trap: the model works on texts unlike those it learned from. A model trained on Reddit can misread a ticket-support complaint, and "sick" in a music forum can mean "great".

Apply it. A fashion brand that counts how many reviews mention "sizing" can lemmatize and drop stop words. The same brand asking whether customers liked the fit must keep negations and context. Review: Before understanding, clean.

6. From a score to a decision: sentiment and emotions

Sentiment analysis classifies the attitude of a text as positive, negative or neutral. Aspect-based sentiment scores each aspect: "beautiful writing, weak ending" is positive on the writing and negative on the ending, which shows what the customer dislikes. Emotion analysis detects states such as joy, fear or anger. "I am afraid the payment went twice" and "You are useless" are both negative, but the first customer needs reassurance and the second needs calming.

A supervised classifier turns the text into a vector of numbers (an embedding), gives one score per class and uses softmax to turn scores into probabilities that sum to 1. Scores z = (positive −1.0, neutral 0.5, negative 2.0): e⁻¹ = 0.368, e^0.5 = 1.649, e² = 7.389, sum 9.406. Probabilities: positive 0.039, neutral 0.175, negative 0.786. The 0.786 says how strongly the model backs the label. It does not say how angry the customer is. With imbalanced classes use macro-F1, the average of each class's F1.

Emotions and RoBERTa. Plutchik's wheel has eight primary emotions in four opposite pairs: joy and sadness, trust and disgust, fear and anger, surprise and anticipation. It is a theoretical framework, not a diagnostic test. Fine-tuning specialises a model already trained on general language. RoBERTa, introduced in 2019, fine-tuned on GoEmotions (Reddit comments with 27 emotions plus "neutral") gives 28 independent scores with the sigmoid, σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^−z). For z = 1.2 the score is 0.77, above a 0.5 threshold, so the label is assigned. For z = −0.4 it is 0.40, so it is not. Softmax makes classes compete; sigmoid asks a yes-or-no question for each emotion, so one text can carry several labels. The scores show confidence, not the intensity of the emotion. The model card reports, at a 0.5 threshold, accuracy 0.474 and F1 0.450, with rare emotions much worse. Measure any public model on your own text. Demszky et al., GoEmotions, 2020 · Liu et al., RoBERTa, 2019 · Model card

A score is not a decision. Four traps sit between a score and an investment.

Trap Typical error Right question
Frequency vs impact "Many complain, so it is the priority" How many people are hit and how much is lost per case?
Sentiment vs behaviour "They are angry, so they will leave" Does what they write predict what they do?
Sample vs reality "The comments speak for everyone" Who writes and who stays silent?
Correlation vs cause "The angry topic is the cause" Does changing the process improve the result?

The flow: find the signal and reread the original text, check the model (sarcasm, mixed emotions, domain), estimate the value (reach, severity, benefit, cost), test the change against a baseline, then fund and review. The manager's closing question: which evidence would change our investment decision? Hypothetical: 300 comments complain about a queue and 12 about double charges. If those 12 stand for 1,200 customers who each lost €80, the charges cost more than 300 annoyed readers. Refund data decides, not comment volume.

Real case: Twitter mood and the stock market, 2011. Bollen, Mao and Zeng analysed nearly 10 million tweets from 2008 and reported that a "calm" mood dimension predicted the daily direction of the Dow Jones with 87.6% accuracy. Funds were launched on the idea. But plain positive or negative polarity did not help, the test covered only about 20 trading days, and the statistical link (Granger) shows one signal precedes another, not that it causes it. Twitter users are not the market. Bollen, Mao and Zeng, 2011

The €50,000 mystery challenge. The group task gives 80 comments from a concert ticketing site and asks for eight priorities that split exactly €50,000. Each priority needs customer IDs, quotes, an expected benefit and a success measure. Challenge the model: some comments are sarcastic or mixed. A topic with few comments, such as accessibility, can still matter because people who cannot buy lose the whole purchase and may not write at all.

Apply it. A music venue sees "queue" as the angriest topic and its CTO proposes €300,000 for more servers. First count abandoned queues and unsold tickets. Then test clearer messages and a held place in line on one sale and compare with a similar sale. Review: A score is not a decision.

7. Automating a process, not a step

Business Process Management (BPM) designs, runs, measures and improves processes. Hyperautomation combines AI, data processing and several automation tools to automate whole processes, keeping people where judgement or approval is needed. Simple automation handles one step, such as copying a form into a table. A tool that copies booking emails into a system does not shorten confirmation time if checking and approving stay manual and queued. Measure time per case, errors, cost per case and manual touches. In a Peerless survey of 139 warehouse professionals, 52% called order fulfilment mostly or fully manual and only 4% highly automated. The answers are self-reported. McCrea, Logistics Management, 2025 · Gartner, Hyperautomation

Four levels, each resting on the previous one: process mining (how did the process really go?), predictive (what will happen?), prescriptive (when to intervene?) and augmented BPM (how does it adapt by itself?).

Process mining rebuilds the real process from digital traces. An event log needs three fields per row: case ID, activity and timestamp. It gives discovery (which paths exist), performance (where time is lost), conformance (where rules are broken) and comparison. Hypothetical: three investment requests show extraction takes minutes but about 30 hours pass before the human check. The bottleneck is the queue, so a stronger extraction AI would not help. Real case: Siemens handles about 30 million order lines a year and found its order-to-cash process running in 923,000 variants. The figures come from the vendor Celonis and were not independently verified. Celonis, Siemens story

Predict, then simulate. Predictive process monitoring estimates how each open case will end ("these 40 orders will probably be late"). Simulation tests a design change before you pay for it, because queues are not linear. Utilisation ρ = λ × s / (60 × c), with λ cases per hour, s minutes per case and c people. With 20 cases an hour, 5 minutes each and 2 people, ρ = 20 × 5 / 120 = 0.83. As ρ nears 1, waiting explodes.

A right prediction can lead to a useless action. The Dutch benefits agency UWV pays about 300,000 people, and roughly 4% each month must repay money, mostly through income-reporting mistakes. Researchers trained a model on 73,153 cases and it worked: the 10% judged most at risk held 19% of all errors. The intervention, an email to people with error probability above 0.8, was chosen by brainstorming. Tested in 2017 on 86,850 cases with a control group, errors were 3.8% with the system and 4.0% without, not a significant difference. Dees et al., 2021 · Dees et al., 2019

Uplift modelling estimates how much an action changes the outcome, not who is at risk. Persuadables act only if contacted, sure things act anyway, lost causes never act, and sleeping dogs do worse if contacted: a reminder can push a distracted subscriber to cancel. Only an experiment with a control group shows the effect. To decide whether to intervene: V = p × u × C − k, with p the predicted probability of a bad outcome, u the share by which the action cuts it (measured in an experiment), C the cost of the bad outcome and k the cost of the action. Hypothetical: p = 0.4, u = 0.25, C = €2,000, k = €50 gives V = 0.4 × 0.25 × 2,000 − 50 = €150, so intervene. With u = 0, V = −€50 on every case, even with a perfect prediction.

ABPMS and framed autonomy. An Augmented BPM System (ABPMS) uses trustworthy AI to reason and act on processes within constraints. Framed autonomy is freedom to decide inside explicit goals, rules and safety limits, such as automatic refunds up to €100. If a strike brings 20,000 refund requests, a good system adapts inside the frame but does not raise the limit itself: changing the frame belongs to the people accountable. Dumas et al., ABPMS manifesto, 2023

Human, automatic, autonomous. Task execution is doing the work; orchestration is coordinating it. A human process has people on both, an automatic one has a process engine and bots, an autonomous one has an ABPMS and agents inside a frame. Responsibility stays with people. In the 2024 Moffatt v. Air Canada ruling the airline argued its chatbot was a separate entity. The tribunal rejected this and ordered it to pay 812.02 Canadian dollars. Klarna reported in 2024 that its AI assistant handled about two-thirds of customer chats, then in 2025 said cost-driven automation had lowered quality and it was hiring humans again. CBC News on Air Canada · Klarna, 2024 · Entrepreneur, 2025

The investor-PDF challenge. A fictional asset manager receives PDF forms. The group extracts, checks, splits money between two funds and computes shares = amount ÷ NAV, where the net asset value (NAV) is the value of one share. Example: $160,600 at a NAV of 10.25 gives 15,668.2927 shares. Ten names repeated five times are namesakes, not duplicates. Fillable PDFs already hold structured fields, so read them directly. A missing NAV or signature must block the case, not be guessed.

Apply it. A streetwear brand running a drop should map orders, fraud check, stock and returns as one process, find from an event log where emails wait for a person, and test any automation with simulation and a control group. Review: Process mining and A right prediction, a useless action.

8. From a concept to a strong exam answer

Whether the test asks for about 400 words or a short paragraph, build the answer around one argument in four blocks.

  1. Claim. Answer in two lines and say what is at stake for the firm.
  2. Concept in your own words. Define the term, then explain how it works, cause to effect.
  3. Real case with facts. Use a dated fact or number. Say what it measures and why it supports the claim.
  4. Limit and recommendation. Name what the evidence cannot show. Recommend a decision the firm can take, with a success measure.

Worked example (practice, not an official question). A ticketing company finds that "queue" is the most negative topic in its comments. Should it spend €300,000 on more servers?

Claim. The company should not approve the €300,000 yet. A negative score shows where customers are unhappy. It does not show what a fix is worth or what causes the problem. A small test comes first.

Concept. Sentiment analysis labels the tone of a text, and an emotion model can add labels such as anger or fear. These scores show how confident the model is about a label. They do not measure how angry customers are, how many are affected or how much money is lost. Four traps separate a score from a decision. Frequency is not impact. What people write is not what they do. Online comments are a sample, not the market. A topic linked to anger is not proven to be its cause. Sarcasm can also flip the tone.

Case. In November 2022 Ticketmaster opened a presale for the Eras Tour. The company said 3.5 million people had registered and the system received 3.5 billion requests, four times its previous peak. The site froze, many fans lost their place and the public sale was cancelled on 17 November (NBC News, 2022). The complaints pointed at a real failure. But capacity, bot filtering and clear messages are different investments, so the fix must match the cause.

Limit and recommendation. Our dashboard cannot say how many buyers left the queue, how many tickets went unsold, or whether anger comes from waiting or from poor messages. I would first count abandoned queues and unsold tickets, and reread a sample of comments for sarcasm. Then I would run one sale with clearer queue messages and a protected place in line, and compare abandonment with a similar sale. If the loss is large and the test shows that delay itself is the cause, the servers are justified. I would judge success by completed purchases and refunds, not by a better sentiment score.

Typical mistakes.

  • Quoting accuracy alone, without the baseline, the class balance or the cost of each error.
  • Using the test set to choose models, or splitting time-ordered data at random.
  • Placing computation in the cloud without checking the decision deadline.
  • Reading a sentiment or emotion score as intensity, or as a decision.
  • Calling an automated step an automated process, or predicting risk without testing the intervention.
  • Defining a term and stopping. Every answer needs a case and a decision.
  • Quoting vendor figures as proven. Say "the company reports" and name the limit.
  • Presenting a hypothetical number as real. Label it hypothetical.

Quiz

Choose an answer. Read the explanation before moving on.

  1. A streetwear brand trains a model to predict which orders will be returned. One input column is 'refund issued: yes or no'. Why is it a problem?

    A feature must be known at prediction time. A refund exists only after the return, so it hides the answer inside the clues. Rarity, data type and price are not the issue.

  2. A festival organiser has three years of weekly ticket sales and wants to forecast the next quarter. How should the test set be built?

    The split must copy reality: the model will predict the future, so it is tested on later periods it has not seen. A random split lets it see the future, and reversing time or reusing the same weeks does not imitate real use.

  3. A model that flags counterfeit listings has TP = 24, FP = 6, FN = 16. What is its F1?

    Precision = 24/30 = 80%. Recall = 24/40 = 60%. F1 = 2 × 0.80 × 0.60 / 1.40 ≈ 68.6%. 70% is the simple average, 48% is the product of the two, and 60% is the recall alone.

  4. A fraud model has 99.2% accuracy. Only 0.8% of transactions are fraud. What do you check first?

    A model that always says 'not fraud' already scores 99.2%. You need recall on the rare class and a comparison with that baseline. The 50% comparison only suits balanced classes, and F1 must be read on the class you care about.

  5. A pricing model tested well on past sales, then loses money after the housing market becomes unstable. Which idea explains it best?

    Drift is a fall in performance because conditions change after training, as in Zillow Offers in 2021. Underfitting would show poor results from the start, and leakage would inflate test scores, not cause a later collapse.

  6. A CEO of a retail group says: 'Margins are falling, build us an AI.' What is the best first move?

    Analytics starts from the decision that must change. Another dashboard repeats the 'dashboards everywhere' problem, and a platform or a churn model chooses technology before the question.

  7. A glass-factory robot must stop within 60 ms of seeing a person. Capture takes 15 ms, inference 20 ms and local action 10 ms. A cloud round trip adds 90 ms. Where should the model run?

    The place of computing follows the deadline. Local total: 15 + 20 + 10 = 45 ms, a margin of 15 ms. With the cloud: 135 ms. Confirmation by the cloud brings the same round trip back, and a buffer protects against outages, not deadlines.

  8. A refrigerated-truck pilot (hypothetical) costs €120k, avoids €70k of losses a year and has €30k of yearly running costs. What are the payback and the payback if avoided losses fall to €50k?

    Net benefit = 70 − 30 = €40k, so payback = 120 / 40 = 3 years. With €50k avoided: net = 20, payback = 120 / 20 = 6 years. Ignoring running costs gives 1.7 and 2.4 years. Scaling costs with losses is wrong, because running costs stay fixed.

  9. In 2016 cameras and video recorders helped knock many sites offline through the Mirai botnet. What weakness did it exploit and what is the lesson for a firm?

    Mirai searched for devices that still used default usernames and passwords. A cheap connected device with no secure identity or updates is a risk for everyone, not only its owner.

  10. A review reads 'The merch was not worth the price.' A pipeline removes stop words with a standard list that includes 'not'. What does the sentiment model most likely see?

    The negation is the signal and the list deletes it. Stop-word removal suits topic counting, not sentiment. Standard lists include 'not', so the sentence is not unchanged, and the pipeline does not reject sentences without verbs.

  11. A support bot answers 'Great, thanks for reminding me the flight is delayed 6 hours' with 'Glad you enjoyed it!'. Which level of analysis failed?

    Words, grammar and literal meaning were fine. What failed is the intention behind the words: the customer is complaining. That is the pragmatic level, helped by discourse.

  12. A classifier gives scores z = (positive 1.0, neutral 0, negative 0). What does softmax give for 'positive'?

    Softmax = e¹ / (e¹ + e⁰ + e⁰) = 2.718 / 4.718 ≈ 0.58. 0.73 is the sigmoid used for independent labels. A zero score is not zero probability, and the classes are not equally likely.

  13. A study reports that a 'calm' mood score from tweets predicts the daily direction of a stock index with 87.6% accuracy. What do you check before launching a fund?

    The test covered only about 20 trading days, the statistical link shows one signal precedes another and not that it causes it, and Twitter users are not the market. Polarity did not even help in the study.

  14. A churn email costs €40 per customer. The bad outcome costs €500, the predicted probability is 0.3 and an experiment shows the email cuts that probability by 20%. Is it worth sending?

    V = p × u × C − k = 0.3 × 0.2 × 500 − 40 = 30 − 40 = −€10. The other options ignore the measured effect of the action or count the full risk as if it were prevented. Uncertainty is already inside p.

  15. An event log of an order process shows data extraction takes 4 minutes but orders then wait about 30 hours for a human check. What should the firm do?

    Process mining places the bottleneck in the queue before the check, not in extraction. Speeding up an unconstrained step does not shorten the cycle, and removing a check may break conformance rules.

Flashcards

Tap a card to flip it. Use the arrow buttons to move through the deck.

  1. Feature or label?
    A feature is a clue the model sees at prediction time. The label is the correct answer known in past data.
  2. Precision or recall?
    Precision: of the alarms, how many are true. Recall: of the true cases, how many are found. Costly misses favour recall. Costly alarms favour precision.
  3. F1 in one line?
    The harmonic mean of precision and recall: 2PR / (P + R). It is high only when both are high.
  4. What is the accuracy trap?
    With rare positives a lazy model has high accuracy and zero recall. 'Nobody cancels' scores 80% when 20% cancel.
  5. Overfitting or underfitting?
    Overfitting: low training error, high error on new data. Underfitting: high error on both.
  6. Validation or test set?
    Validation is for choosing models and hyperparameters. The test set is opened once, at the end.
  7. What is data leakage?
    Using information the model would not have at prediction time. The score looks great and is false.
  8. What happened at Zillow Offers?
    Model drift: in 2021 the market changed faster than the pricing model. The board closed the business and a $304 million write-down followed.
  9. Decision tree or k-means?
    A tree learns from labelled answers and predicts. K-means finds groups with no labels, and you choose K.
  10. Hub-and-spoke or centralised team?
    Centralised gives standards but a queue. Hub-and-spoke keeps shared definitions at the centre and analysts near each business area.
  11. Edge or cloud?
    Edge for decisions inside a deadline. Cloud for history, whole-fleet analysis and training models.
  12. Where do you train and where do you infer?
    Train and improve in the cloud on fleet data. Run inference at the edge, with tested, signed and reversible updates.
  13. MW or MWh?
    MW is power right now. MWh is energy over a stated period: 3 MW for 2 hours is 6 MWh.
  14. Payback period?
    Investment / net annual benefit. €300k / €180k ≈ 1.7 years. Running costs stay fixed, so a halved benefit can triple the payback.
  15. What is the lesson of Mirai?
    Cheap devices with default passwords and no updates endanger everyone. Require unique identity, signed updates and rollback.
  16. What is store-and-forward buffering?
    Keeping priority events during a network outage and sending them when the connection returns. Test the size of the buffer.
  17. Stemming or lemmatization?
    Stemming cuts endings with rules (skipping to skip). Lemmatization uses grammar and a dictionary (are, is to be).
  18. Why not always remove stop words?
    Lists often include 'not'. 'The show was not good' becomes 'show good', which hides the negation.
  19. Softmax or sigmoid?
    Softmax: classes compete and sum to 1. Sigmoid: independent yes-or-no scores, so one text can carry several emotions.
  20. Sentiment or emotion?
    Sentiment is the attitude: positive, negative, neutral. Emotion is a specific state such as joy, fear or anger.
  21. A score or a decision?
    Check frequency vs impact, sentiment vs behaviour, sample vs reality, and correlation vs cause before spending.
  22. Automate a step or a process?
    A step is one task. Hyperautomation links the whole chain, handovers and exceptions included, and keeps people where judgement is needed.
  23. What does an event log need?
    Case ID, activity and timestamp for every event. Process mining uses it to find paths, delays and rule breaches.
  24. Persuadables or sleeping dogs?
    Persuadables act only if contacted. Sleeping dogs do worse if contacted. Only a control group shows which is which.
  25. Who answers for an autonomous agent?
    The organisation. In the 2024 Air Canada ruling the airline was held responsible for its chatbot. Framed autonomy keeps decisions inside rules people set.
