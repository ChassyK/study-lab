How to study this guide

Allow 45–50 minutes for reading and study. Use about 25 minutes for origins, power, security and supply chains, 12 minutes for the 9 November topics and 8 minutes for the worked answer and review. Pause after each chapter to explain how the risk reaches a firm. The quiz and flashcards come after the reading.

Course sources. The 2 November lesson and 16 November lesson draw on partial recordings and course slides. This guide also uses the slides on geopolitical risks and business obligations and the assigned World Economic Forum report. The 9 November lesson reconstructs cognitive warfare from the lecturer's reference on 16 November and documented examples. Ethics and globalization come from the course outline and assigned readings as syllabus-based review. The real-world applications below extend the course concepts. Check Canvas for official assessment instructions.

A state is a political authority governing a territory. Geopolitics studies how geography and competition for power shape decisions. Geopolitical risk is the possibility that those decisions affect a firm's costs, sales, safety or ability to operate. Ask who acts, what they want, what tool they use, and how it reaches a firm. Exposure is the part of the business that the tool can reach. Start with one chain of cause and effect.

1. When a firm's nationality becomes a risk

Your home country is where your company originates. The host country is where it operates abroad. Liability of foreignness means the extra cost of being an outsider. A new retailer abroad may need time to learn local rules and build supplier relationships. Liability of origin is the disadvantage attached to a particular home country. When relations with that country deteriorate, its firms can become targets.

How it works. A host government can apply informal coercion, pressure without a formal sanction, through inspections, unwanted delays or cancelled events. A boycott is a refusal to buy or participate in order to exert pressure. Local consumers, platforms and distributors can add to the effect.

Real case from the lesson, with a K-pop connection. In 2017 South Korean retailer Lotte provided land for deployment of the US-made THAAD missile-defence system. China opposed the deployment. Dozens of Lotte stores in China were closed, officially for safety reasons. Events featuring South Korean musicians and actors were also suspended or cancelled. A dispute about security reached retail and entertainment through their association with South Korea. US–China Economic and Security Review Commission, 2017, pp. 343–345

Apply it. A promoter can build local embeddedness through local crews, distributors and partners who also benefit if the show goes ahead. Structural separation uses distinct operating units, contracts or governance to contain disruption within one market. The links to the parent firm can still matter. Spread revenue across markets and agree in advance who pays if an event is cancelled.

2. G-Zero, asymmetric threats and chokepoints

G-Zero, an idea from Bremmer and Roubini, describes a world where no country or bloc has both the ability and willingness to lead globally. A bloc is a group of states linked by interests or rules. The Group of Seven (G7) groups advanced economies, the Group of Twenty (G20) includes a wider set of economies, and a permanent member of the UN Security Council can use a veto to block a substantive decision. Cooperation continues, but firms cannot assume a fast, unified response to a crisis. Bremmer and Roubini, A G-Zero World

An asymmetric threat exploits an imbalance. A small actor can cause large disruption with far fewer resources than a major power has. The attribution problem is the difficulty of proving who was responsible. Ask who acted, who may have supported them and what the evidence shows. In Crimea in 2014, forces without insignia obscured responsibility while Russia took control. Escalation is a rise in a dispute's scope or intensity as actors react.

Deterrence seeks to prevent an action by making an opponent expect it will fail or cost too much. Russia's invasion of Ukraine exposed Europe's energy and security dependencies. Under Article 5 of the North Atlantic Treaty, allies consider an armed attack against one an attack against all. Each takes the action it deems necessary, which can include armed force. Finland and Sweden joined NATO in 2023 and 2024. NATO enlargement · North Atlantic Treaty

A chokepoint is a place or capability where a large flow has few alternatives. Bab el-Mandeb and Suez lie on the route between Asia and Europe. Hormuz matters for energy shipments, Taiwan for advanced chip capacity. Concentration can also occur in mineral extraction, refining or the infrastructure that moves goods. Proximate factors are the immediate triggers, such as a group threatening shipping. Structural factors are persistent conditions, such as dependence on one route or a few producers.

Real logistics case for streetwear. Attacks on ships in the Red Sea led many carriers to divert. UN Trade and Development (UNCTAD) reported that monthly Suez ship transits had fallen 42% from their peak by January 2024. Shipping around southern Africa adds time and distance. A streetwear brand that schedules a drop before its goods arrive may waste marketing spend and miss sales. Compare the margin lost from late stock with the cost of an alternative route or extra inventory. UNCTAD, Navigating Troubled Waters

3. Three kinds of power and the tools states use

Geostrategy uses geography and strategic capabilities such as military forces, alliances and bases. Geoeconomics uses trade, finance, investment or access to markets to pursue strategic goals. Geoculture looks at the influence of culture, identity and narratives. Soft power is influence through attraction. A country can attract interest through films. A government that blocks a film uses regulatory power to control distribution.

Real anime and gaming case. Japan's Cool Japan strategy names manga, anime and video games as sources of international interest. Fans can turn that interest into travel, sales or licensing, permission to use a work or brand on agreed terms. A publisher can pair a localized game with a merchandise partner. The popularity of a series can help it find an audience even if views of Japanese government policy vary. Japan Cabinet Office, New Cool Japan Strategy

A tariff is a tax on imports. The importer normally pays customs and may pass some of the cost to customers, absorb it or negotiate with suppliers. A sanction restricts dealings to pressure, deter or limit a target. An asset freeze restricts the use of assets. An embargo blocks or limits trade with a target. A countersanction is a restriction imposed in response. Tariffs can also be used as political leverage. Identify what the tool does before explaining its objective.

Conflicting rules. In July 2025 the EU targeted two Chinese banks in a Russia-related package. China imposed countermeasures on two Lithuanian banks in August. China's Anti-Foreign Sanctions Law provides instruments against certain foreign restrictive measures it regards as discriminatory. A firm may face competing demands across markets. Map the banks, entities, transactions and jurisdictions, legal authorities that can regulate them, before entering a contract. China Ministry of Commerce, countermeasures announcement

4. National security, chips, data and investment

National security means protecting a state's essential interests. Today that may include energy, communications, infrastructure, data and technology alongside military defence. Securitization is the process of presenting an issue as a security threat and using that framing to justify stronger controls. Dual use means one product or body of knowledge can serve civilian and military or security purposes. Health data can support treatment and research, while access to large sensitive datasets can also raise security concerns. The historical US 2018 Nuclear Posture Review illustrates the broader framing. It discussed possible responses in extreme circumstances, including certain serious non-nuclear strategic attacks.

Small yard, high fence means choosing a limited set of sensitive technologies and controlling them tightly. An export control restricts transfers of specified goods, software or technology. A licence gives official permission for a covered transfer. Lithography prints patterns onto chips. EUV uses extreme ultraviolet light for fine patterns. DUV uses deep ultraviolet light and can achieve some fine features with extra steps. Older equipment, suppliers in other countries and import substitution, replacing imported goods with domestic ones, can weaken a control's effect. A 2023 teardown found an advanced SMIC chip in Huawei's Mate 60 Pro. That shows progress on that chip. Cost, scale and access to other components are separate questions. TechInsights teardown

Real gaming and AI case. In October 2023 NVIDIA disclosed that new US licence requirements covered products including the RTX 4090 in certain markets. A GPU, or graphics processing unit, runs many calculations at once. That makes it useful for graphics and some AI tasks. The gaming label alone could not settle whether rules applied. A chip company should review product specifications, destination and ultimate user, then allow for licensing delays in its sales forecast. NVIDIA filing

Separate the controls by what moves.

  • Inbound investment screening reviews ownership or capital entering a country. The US Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States (CFIUS) reviews some acquisitions, minority investments with specified rights, and real estate deals near sensitive sites. Mitigation is an agreed measure to lower risk. Divestment means selling an asset. In 2024 the US ordered MineOne to sell property near a military base. US Treasury, MineOne
  • Outbound investment controls concern capital and associated benefits going abroad. US rules cover certain investments in advanced chips, quantum technology and AI. The Foreign Direct Product Rule can bring some foreign-made goods within US export rules when specified links to US technology or equipment and other conditions exist. Identify whether the company is investing or moving an item. US Treasury, outbound program · US Commerce, export rule
  • Data and infrastructure rules can restrict access to large sets of sensitive personal data or to certain connected hardware and software. A connected vehicle sends and receives data over networks. A drone is an aircraft operated with nobody on board. Suppliers and software updates can create exposure after purchase. State-level rules can add limits on land, public procurement (government buying) and public pension investments. US DOJ, data rule · US Commerce, connected vehicles

5. Rule of law and cognitive warfare

Rule of law means that rules bind people, firms and governments and can be enforced by independent courts. Checks and balances are ways institutions limit one another's power. Businesses need reliable contracts and a way to challenge decisions. Vague intelligence or data rules can create a chilling effect. A firm avoids even lawful audits or research because consequences are uncertain. An exit ban is a legal restriction on a person leaving a country. Assess risks to staff and data as well as financial losses. For example, a game publisher may delay transferring player data if a new rule is vague and there is no reliable way to appeal an enforcement decision.

Cognitive warfare uses deliberate, coordinated actions to shape how an audience interprets information and makes decisions for strategic advantage. A narrative gives meaning to facts or claims. Disinformation is deliberately misleading information. False misinformation can spread without intent to mislead. A false story can change trust, sales or a partner's decisions before facts are established. The 1999 book Unrestricted Warfare is course context for studying how economic, media and other tools can be used in conflict. Attribution of a real event requires evidence about that event. NATO Allied Command Transformation, cognitive warfare

Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference (FIMI) is the EU's term for intentionally manipulative and coordinated behaviour in the information environment with a foreign link. Check the factual claim, evidence of intent and evidence of authorship separately. Lateral reading means leaving the original post to check independent sources. In 2013 a false post from Associated Press's compromised Twitter account claimed that the White House had been attacked. The Dow Jones stock-market index fell by more than 150 points and quickly recovered. A credible-looking signal changed decisions before it was checked. Build cognitive resilience by requiring independent confirmation for costly decisions. While checking a report of a port closure, hold an alternative booking you can cancel before you commit to a diversion. EU External Action Service, FIMI · Associated Press, market reaction

Real film case. In 2014 a hack against Sony Pictures and threats connected to The Interview disrupted its release. The FBI attributed the attack to North Korea. This shows asymmetric pressure on distribution and how audience and partner decisions matter. The attribution rests on investigative evidence. A studio should verify threats and assess physical safety, communications and distribution separately. FBI, Sony investigation

6. Corruption and corporate responsibility

The ethics topics here and in the next chapter are syllabus-based review.

Corruption is abuse of entrusted power for private gain. Bribery means offering, giving, soliciting or accepting an improper benefit to influence a decision. A facilitation payment speeds up a routine official act. The US FCPA has a narrow exception for certain routine government actions that do not involve deciding whether to award or continue business. The UK Bribery Act has no facilitation-payment exception, and local law or company policy may also prohibit the payment. US DOJ, FCPA guide · UK Crown Prosecution Service, Bribery Act guidance Pervasiveness is how common corrupt demands are. Arbitrariness is how unpredictable the demands or outcomes are. A bribe buys a favour which can be withdrawn. Calling it a hidden tax explains the cost, but an actual published tax has more predictable rules and formal procedures.

Extraterritoriality means a rule can apply to conduct abroad if the required legal connection exists. The US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA) targets covered bribery of foreign officials and imposes accounting obligations on covered issuers. The UK Bribery Act includes a corporate failure-to-prevent offence. The OECD Anti-Bribery Convention, negotiated through the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, commits its parties to implementing foreign bribery rules through domestic law. Italy's Legislative Decree 231/2001 can impose liability on entities for specified offences under defined conditions. In the Siemens case, coordinated enforcement announced in 2008 resulted in about $1.6 billion in penalties across jurisdictions. US Department of Justice, Siemens

A third party is an outsider who deals with or acts for the firm, such as a local agent. Due diligence checks who owns it, what service it provides, what it charges and whether its records agree. A compliance program puts rules into everyday work through approval, training, reporting and follow-up. Reputation borrowing means working with a credible partner whose standards and scrutiny make your commitments more believable. The firm still owns its decisions.

Real cinema case. US prosecutors alleged that proceeds diverted from Malaysia's 1MDB state fund helped finance The Wolf of Wall Street. Here the concern is stolen funds and financial governance. A film producer should verify the original source of funds, identify who ultimately owns or controls the company sending them, and compare the intermediary's account to actual records. US Department of Justice, 1MDB allegations

7. Institutions, rights and backlash against globalization

Institutions are the rules and organizations that structure decisions. Governance asks who makes decisions, who checks them and what happens when rules are broken. Foreign aid is cross-border support for goals such as development or recovery. Conditionality ties support to requirements such as safeguards or reforms. The Marshall Plan helped fund European recovery after World War II. Its context matters. Where public institutions cannot track spending, donors need local oversight and measurable results. William Easterly's planners versus searchers distinguishes designing a project centrally from learning what works locally. Debarment excludes firms from eligible contracts after specified misconduct. US State Department, Marshall Plan · World Bank sanctions system

Corporate responsibility includes investigating and improving effects on workers and communities. Under apartheid, South Africa's system of legally enforced racial segregation, the voluntary Sullivan Principles set workplace standards for participating firms. These rules could improve workplaces. Changing a political system required wider action. In 1985 artists from rap, rock and reggae backed Sun City, a public refusal to play at an apartheid-era entertainment complex. A promoter can apply that logic by setting labour standards for venues and giving workers a way to report breaches. UN Centre Against Apartheid, Artists and Entertainers Against Apartheid, p. 7

Globalization is deeper cross-border integration of trade, production, finance and people. Hyperglobalization is the push for particularly deep integration, including rules that can narrow domestic policy choices. Backlash is political resistance. The China shock was a surge in Chinese import competition with uneven impacts on local labour markets. Consumers can gain from lower prices while workers in exposed towns face lasting job losses.

Populism frames politics as ordinary people against a corrupt or detached elite. Rodrik distinguishes what drives voters (income and job shocks, or culture and identity concerns) from how parties mobilize them (economic proposals or cultural messages). Economic shocks can work through identity. A brand benefiting from low-cost imports should study whether a political reaction could change tariffs, contracts or its reputation. The strength of that explanation depends on evidence for each place. Autor, Dorn and Hanson, The China Shock · Rodrik, Why Does Globalization Fuel Populism?

8. Designing a supply chain for political risk

A supply chain moves inputs and products towards customers. A global value chain includes cross-border activities that add value, from design and manufacturing to marketing and service. A firm in a large domestic market may depend less on exports. An export-led business is more exposed to foreign demand and trade barriers. Exports as a share of GDP, the value of final output produced domestically, give one clue. For a business decision, measure its own revenue and input dependencies. RCEP (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership) in the Asia-Pacific and USMCA (United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement) in North America reduce certain barriers between participants.

Efficiency keeps costs and waste low. Resilience means absorbing disruption and recovering operations. Diversification spreads dependencies. Adaptability is the ability to switch in time. Buffer stock is inventory reserved for a disruption. Reshoring brings an activity home, nearshoring moves it nearby, and friend-shoring moves it toward politically aligned countries. De-risking selectively reduces exposure. Decoupling separates economic relationships more broadly. Two suppliers offer limited protection if both use the same factory or route.

Politics as a design parameter means treating rules as an input to design, like price or delivery time. The assigned WEF–Kearney report cites more than 3,000 new trade and industrial-policy measures in 2025, about 3.5 times the annual total in 2016. Industrial policy steers production or investment towards government priorities. The report models four situations that can coexist.

Outlook What a firm might see
Transactional Market access depends more on negotiated deals and conditions.
Volatile Repeated shocks and sudden rule changes undermine fixed plans.
Fragmented Rival blocs follow different rules and supplier networks.
Degraded Trade and coordination weaken enough to cause recurring breakdowns.

Orchestration coordinates suppliers, carriers and other partners around shared rules and information. Distributed scale keeps large combined output through multiple coordinated sites. Optionality makes it possible to switch routes, suppliers or sites in time. A backup supplier that needs six months of testing may arrive too late. Use a simple risk map. Consider current evidence, immediate triggers and structural causes against security, legal and ethical risks. Then decide who tracks the risk and when they will act. WEF and Kearney, Global Value Chains Outlook 2026, pp. 3–7

Real sneaker case. Nike reported 97 finished-footwear factories in 11 countries for fiscal 2025, yet Vietnam produced about 51% of Nike Brand footwear. Factories in one country share some of the same exposure. A brand can map its factories, component suppliers and shipping routes, then approve a second source in advance. Compare the extra annual cost with expected losses and with the impact of a severe disruption. Measure time to switch as well as price. Nike annual filing, fiscal 2025

9. From a concept to a strong exam answer

The first midterm used an English blog-post answer of about 400 words. Use it as a practice format. Build four paragraphs around one argument.

  1. Claim. Answer the question and state what is at stake for the firm.
  2. Concept and mechanism. Define the term, name the actor and explain how pressure reaches the business.
  3. Evidence. Use a dated fact or figure. Explain what it measures and how it supports the claim.
  4. Decision and trade-off. Recommend a concrete response, say what it costs or cannot solve, and name a measure of success.

Worked example. A brand with a fixed drop date should value the option to switch shipping routes as well as transport price. A chokepoint is a point where a large flow has few alternatives. During the Red Sea disruption, monthly Suez transits fell 42% from their peak by January 2024. If sneakers arrive after the advertised date, a low shipping price can lead to a bigger sales loss. A brand could reserve alternative capacity for limited releases and hold a small stock buffer for essentials. Both options cost money. I would test whether the avoided margin loss and better on-time delivery justify the premium. The Suez figure is evidence. The sneaker scenario applies it to a business decision.

If the question is about a threat, say who can cause it and through what channel. If it is about a law, name what it restricts. If it asks for a recommendation, name a decision a firm can actually make and how it will judge the result.

Quiz

Choose an answer. Read the explanation before moving on.

  1. A government repeatedly inspects firms from one country after a military dispute. Local and other foreign firms face no change. What best explains the pattern?

    The exposure is tied to a particular home country and a change in relations. A general knowledge gap faced by foreign firms would be liability of foreignness.

  2. A shipping group cannot tell whether a disruption was accidental, carried out by a militia or ordered by a government. Which question comes first?

    This is an attribution problem. A group claiming responsibility and a government directing the action are different claims and require different evidence.

  3. Two sneaker suppliers are in different countries, but both ship through the same disrupted strait. What should the brand test first?

    Diversification only protects against the dependencies it actually changes. Both suppliers are exposed to the same route chokepoint.

  4. A manga gains international fans and generates travel and licensing demand. Which concept most directly explains the initial interest?

    People voluntarily engage with the culture. Commercial spillovers can follow even when people disagree with the country's foreign policy.

  5. A state freezes a foreign bank's assets to pressure its government. The target state restricts dealings with two banks in response. What happened?

    The first action restricts dealings to exert pressure and the second responds in kind. A tariff is a tax collected on imports.

  6. A GPU sold for gaming can also support advanced computing. A regulator restricts shipments to certain markets. What is the mechanism?

    The chip has different potential uses. The instrument governs cross-border transfer based on features and applicable rules, even when a product is marketed for gaming.

  7. A foreign investor acquires land close to a sensitive site. The host government orders a resale. Which terms apply?

    The transaction brings foreign ownership into the host jurisdiction. Requiring the investor to sell the asset is divestment.

  8. A studio hears a viral claim about the source of threats against a film. Which response best manages cognitive and operational risk?

    Attribution needs evidence. A studio can protect staff and prepare distribution options while it verifies who was behind the threats.

  9. Five accounts repeat a claim that a port is closed. They all link to the same post. What should a logistics manager do before diverting a shipment?

    Lateral reading checks the report with sources outside the original channel. Repeating the same post does not add independent confirmation. Hold a reversible backup if delay is costly.

  10. An intermediary asks for another unrecorded payment after an earlier one, and offers no reliable decision in return. Which concept explains the planning problem?

    Arbitrariness concerns uncertainty about the amount, timing, recipient and result. An earlier payment gives the business no enforceable right.

  11. A firm argues that a foreign intermediary shields it from liability at home. Which response is best?

    Rules can apply to covered conduct abroad. Due diligence checks a third party's identity, service, payment and documentation rather than relying on a promise.

  12. A multinational introduces workplace rights standards in a country with discriminatory laws. What is the most credible expectation?

    A firm can improve its workplaces and exert pressure through partners. Changing national institutions usually requires broader political and legal action.

  13. Cheaper imports benefit shoppers, but factory job losses are concentrated in a few towns. What helps explain new trade barriers becoming politically attractive?

    Aggregate gains can coexist with persistent local losses. Parties may mobilize affected groups using economic and identity messages.

  14. A second shoe factory has qualification approval and can start within weeks if needed. Which WEF concept best describes that ability?

    Optionality means a realistic ability to switch in time. Two sites may contribute to distributed scale, but the question hinges on a usable option.

  15. An essay says that geopolitical risk is rising and lists six definitions. What would improve the answer most?

    A convincing application links an actor and tool to a specific consequence. It then recommends a measurable response and explains a trade-off.

Flashcards

Tap a card to flip it. Use the arrow buttons to move through the deck.

  1. Foreignness or origin?
    Foreignness is a general outsider disadvantage. Origin is a disadvantage tied to the firm's particular home country.
  2. Local embeddedness and structural separation
    Local embeddedness builds ties in the host economy. Structural separation limits the spread of a shock across operating units.
  3. What is G-Zero?
    No country or bloc is both able and willing to lead globally. Cooperation can still work in specific regions or on individual issues.
  4. Asymmetric threat and attribution
    The threat exploits an imbalance. Attribution asks who was responsible and what evidence supports that conclusion.
  5. Deterrence and NATO Article 5
    Deterrence discourages an action by raising expected costs or lowering chances of success. Under Article 5 each ally takes action it deems necessary.
  6. Proximate vs structural risk
    Proximate factors are immediate drivers. Structural factors are long-lasting exposures, such as reliance on one route.
  7. What makes an asset a chokepoint?
    Many flows depend on it and alternatives are scarce or slow to use. Suez transits dropped during the Red Sea disruption.
  8. Geostrategy, geoeconomics and geoculture
    Geostrategy uses geographical and strategic capabilities, geoeconomics uses economic tools, and geoculture concerns cultural and narrative influence.
  9. Soft power in anime or gaming
    It is influence through attraction. A successful series can increase interest in merchandise, travel or language.
  10. Tariff, sanction and countersanction
    A tariff taxes imports. A sanction restricts dealings to pressure or limit a target. A countersanction is a response.
  11. Securitization and dual use
    Securitization frames an issue as a security threat. Dual use means a civilian and a military or security application can coexist.
  12. Small yard, high fence
    Select a narrow set of sensitive technologies and control them tightly. Availability from other suppliers and domestic substitutes can weaken the controls.
  13. Inbound, outbound or export control?
    Inbound concerns ownership or capital entering. Outbound concerns covered investment abroad. Export controls govern certain items and transfers.
  14. CFIUS and divestment
    CFIUS reviews certain US inward investment and real estate transactions for national-security risk. Divestment is sale of an asset.
  15. Rule of law and chilling effect
    Rule of law binds governments and gives independent courts a role. A chilling effect occurs when uncertainty discourages even lawful activity.
  16. Cognitive warfare, disinformation and misinformation
    Cognitive warfare targets interpretation and decisions. Disinformation deliberately misleads. Misinformation can spread without intent.
  17. FIMI, lateral reading and reversible decisions
    FIMI concerns deliberate manipulative interference with a foreign link. Lateral reading checks outside the original channel. A reversible decision protects operations while evidence is gathered.
  18. Corruption, bribery and arbitrariness
    Corruption abuses entrusted power. Bribery trades improper benefits for influence. Arbitrariness means demands or results are unpredictable.
  19. Extraterritoriality and third-party due diligence
    A law may reach conduct abroad if a required connection exists. Due diligence checks who owns a partner, what it does and how it is paid.
  20. Aid conditionality and corporate responsibility
    Conditionality links aid to requirements. Corporate responsibility concerns business effects on workers and communities, including beyond the firm's direct operations.
  21. Why can globalization drive backlash?
    Gains and losses can fall on different groups. Concentrated local harm and identity concerns can shape what voters and parties demand.
  22. Resilience, diversification and friend-shoring
    Resilience is absorbing and recovering from shocks. Diversification spreads exposure. Friend-shoring favours politically aligned markets.
  23. Four WEF outlooks
    Transactional means more deal-by-deal access. Volatile means repeated shocks. Fragmented means rival rules. Degraded means breakdowns and shortages.
  24. Orchestration, distributed scale and optionality
    Coordinate partners, spread coordinated production across sites, and create the option to switch in time.
  25. Four parts of the practice answer
    A direct claim, the causal mechanism, a dated real case, and a concrete recommendation with a trade-off and a measure.
